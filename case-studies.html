A service, turned into a product

MyDressboxProductisationCR

Wedding dress cleaning is a quoted service. You ring a dry cleaner, they look at the gown, they name a price. That caps you at the customers near you and makes every sale a conversation.

MyDressbox sells the same work as five fixed prices, bought online like any other product, with the box posted out. The tier keys off the gown itself - style, length, circumference, volume, detailing - so the price can be set before anyone has seen it.

The hard part was never the website. It was deciding what varies and what doesn't, then being willing to fix a price on something you haven't inspected. That is the productisation question in every service business, and it is almost always a commercial decision wearing an operational disguise.

An operating system for a physical workflow

MyDressboxDress|OSTL

Gowns moved through the laundry on a paper log. Nobody could say where a particular dress was without walking the floor, and how long each stage actually took was recorded nowhere.

Dress|OS replaced it. Eleven stages, a scanner that advances a gown rather than merely finding it, timers on the benches where the work happens, photographs at the points where evidence matters later. The people doing the work maintain their own procedures in the app, without waiting on a developer.

The output that matters isn't the software, it's the measurements - what a gown really costs in minutes, where the queue backs up, which quality step is leaking. You cannot improve a process nobody has ever timed, and most businesses this size have never timed one.

Conversion work that starts with a diagnosis

MyDressboxCROMN

Two storefronts across Australia and New Zealand: location pages, product pages, paid landing pages, and the tracking underneath them.

The part worth stealing is the order of operations. Before changing a page, establish whether the page is the problem. Cold traffic arriving with the wrong intent is not fixed by a better headline, and a tracking setup that double counts will have you optimising toward a number that isn't real. Both turned up here. Neither would have been visible to anyone briefed to "improve conversion".

Cognito Crayons

In progressBranding · Website · GTMTL

A hexagonal crayon, hand poured in small batches in New Zealand. Six flat sides, twenty millimetres across, so it stays where a small child puts it. Brand, storefront and route to market.

Two buyers with equal weight and nothing in common. A parent arrives from social with ninety seconds and needs to believe the colour is strong and the stick survives a fist. A teacher arrives needing something requisitionable: a price break, a printable programme, a GST invoice. One store, two paths, neither diluted into a compromise that serves nobody.

The brand's sharpest decision was a list of banned words. Natural, plant based, food grade, edible - the entire vocabulary the category reaches for by reflex. They invite a parent to think of a crayon as food, which is the wrong thought about a thing a toddler will absolutely put in their mouth. The line is that it is certified non toxic and boring enough that it won't stay there. It is a drawing tool, not a snack.

Built on Shopify, synced from Git, with the conversion rules written into the brief rather than left to taste: one primary action per screen, objections answered in the order people actually raise them, and every unanswered question left visible in square brackets instead of quietly invented.

On the absent numbers. Several of these produced results I'm confident about and happy to talk through with the workings.
